# Choose Your Game Before Chasing Alpha

*Not financial advice. This report summarises published research and podcast material for educational purposes. It does not recommend any product, broker or trade. Leveraged products can lose money fast, and UK tax treatment depends on your circumstances. Take regulated advice before acting on anything here.*

For a UK retail trader, the most reliable way to find alpha is to stop competing where most retail traders compete. The evidence is consistent across decades and countries: active retail traders lose on average, and losses grow with turnover, leverage and shorter product life. Among people who day trade persistently, only about **1–3% are reliably profitable**, and **roughly 60–80% of UK retail CFD accounts lose money**. Both podcast guests say edge has moved away from access to information. Kirk McKeown's version is that it now sits in process design, differentiated inputs and synthesis. Alix Pasquet's version is behavioural edge and "game selection", which he sums up as "play weaker players." For an individual, that points to long horizons, low turnover, capacity-constrained niches, real domain knowledge and strict process, not fast directional bets on liquid markets. Commodities are not an easier playground. The opponents are physical trading houses and CTAs, roll and gap risk are severe, and what retail can realistically harvest there is well-known risk premia (trend, carry), not a hidden informational edge. An AI trading system is worth building if it works as a research, engineering and discipline engine: extracting structure from text, writing and checking backtest code, logging every trial, and enforcing risk rules. It should not be an autonomous oracle. The evidence says LLM return predictions decay quickly, are inflated by look-ahead bias, and have mostly failed to beat buy-and-hold in clean tests.

## About 70% of retail accounts lose, and the causes are known

The starting point is the base rate. In Barber and Odean's study of **66,465 US brokerage households**, the most active fifth earned **11.4% a year net against a market return of 17.9%**. Heavy traders underperformed low-turnover households by about **80bp a month** on a risk-adjusted basis, mostly because of costs ([Barber & Odean 2000](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/individual_investor_performance_final.pdf)). Taiwan's full trade records from 1992 to 2006 show day traders losing about **23.9bp per day after fees**. Fewer than **3%** were "predictably profitable", and **95.3%** of experienced losers kept trading ([Barber et al.](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/Day%20Traders/Day%20Trading%20and%20Learning%20110217.pdf)). In Brazil, **97%** of mini-index futures day traders who lasted more than 300 days lost money, and only **1.1%** earned more than the minimum wage. The authors call day trading for a living "virtually impossible" ([Chague et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)).

The same pattern appears in newer products. Retail SPX 0DTE option traders lost more than **$125m** between 2021 and 2023, and over **$90m of that was transaction costs**, not wrong direction ([Beckmeyer, Branger & Gayda](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/fofi2024/files/2024/04/FoFI-2024-146-Leander-Gayda.pdf)). A separate trade-journal dataset gives a useful nuance. Non-0DTE retail option trades placed with limit orders came out roughly flat, while **0DTE trades lost 4.6% per trade** ([Bogousslavsky & Muravyev](https://www.lsu.edu/business/files/event-files/2025-finance-mardi-gras/retail_option_trading_v2.pdf)). Options are not inherently destructive, but short-dated lottery-like ones are. Herding costs money too: the stocks Robinhood users bought most heavily went on to deliver **−4.7% abnormal returns over 20 days** ([Barber et al. 2022](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3715077)). Retail crypto-app users bought into rallies and crashes while large holders sold ([BIS Bulletin 69](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull69.htm)).

UK numbers follow the same shape. When ESMA intervened in 2018 it found that **74–89% of retail CFD accounts** typically lost money. It then capped leverage (30:1 for major FX, 20:1 for gold and major indices, 10:1 for other commodities, 5:1 for single stocks, 2:1 for crypto), required a 50% margin close-out and negative balance protection, and made firms publish the share of accounts that lose ([ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/press-news/esma-news/esma-agrees-prohibit-binary-options-and-restrict-cfds-protect-retail-investors)). The FCA made these rules permanent in COBS 22.5, and they cover spread bets as well ([FCA Handbook](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/cobs22/cobs22s5)). Current disclosures still put most providers at **60–76% losing accounts** ([Good Money Guide](https://goodmoneyguide.com/trading/risk-warning-loss-percentages/)). Two caveats apply. A widely cited "~80% FCA figure" comes second-hand and I could not find the original. Accounts that are simply opted up to professional status fall outside these leverage caps ([Investment Week](https://www.investmentweek.co.uk/feature/4522880/isa-savers-exposed-risky-cfd-products-regulator-raises-consumer-duty-concerns)).

A useful way to read all this is: **net P&L ≈ gross edge − spread − commission/financing − behavioural leakage**. For most retail traders gross edge is close to zero, because the counterparty is usually better informed, so each additional trade is another cost that compounds. Fund investors show the same effect in slow motion. Morningstar estimates investors' dollar-weighted returns trail their funds by about **1.2 percentage points a year**, and by 2pp or more in volatile funds ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap)). Researchers dispute how large that timing cost really is ([Fulkerson et al., FAJ 2026](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4904652)), but the direction is not in question. Most retail traders are not missing a better indicator. They trade too often, use too much leverage, trade products that are too short-dated, follow the crowd, and have never checked whether they have an edge at all.

## McKeown and Pasquet agree edge has moved from data to process and game selection

**Kirk McKeown** worked at Tudor and Glenview and spent about 8.5 years running proprietary research at Point72. He now runs the alt-data firm Carbon Arc. According to the episode's show notes, he argues that alpha "migrates as market structure, tooling, and competition evolve". He says it "often lives in process design, information capture, and interpretation of small narrative inflections", and that as models become commodities the advantage shifts to "differentiated inputs and synthesis" ([Odds on Open Substack](https://ethankho.substack.com/p/alpha-comes-from-a-differentiated-735); [Apple Podcasts](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/alpha-comes-from-a-differentiated-view-ex-point72/id1802640849?i=1000751676477)). The chapter list gives more detail. There are chapters on why "sample size beats anecdotes" in crowded markets and on "domain context plus data" as the durable advantage. Another describes research broken down by market structure and business model, with decision trees whose priors get pruned over time. He also treats **inventory as "the core causal variable behind boom-bust cycles"** ([Substack](https://ethankho.substack.com/p/alpha-comes-from-a-differentiated-735)). He describes research's job narrowly: to "lift idea flow, hit-rate, or conviction without contaminating decision-making." His thesis that "Main Street becomes Wall Street" says data is being "decimalised" and priced like a utility, which flattens informational advantages ([RSS show notes](https://api.substack.com/feed/podcast/spotify/d2a05c69-5eb0-49eb-9468-33890bb7fef6/7804320.rss)). *Other interviews* make the same point. He says the moat is shifting "from access to data towards the architecture used to structure it" ([AI Street](https://www.ai-street.co/p/five-minutes-with-kirk-mckeown-co)), and Carbon Arc offers alt data through an MCP server that an AI-assistant user can query ([Ted Merz](https://ted-merz.com/2026/05/15/view-from-the-office-kirk-mckeown/)). Reports of a roughly **$20/month retail tier are unverified**, and I could not confirm it has launched. Bear in mind that McKeown sells data, so his view that data is levelling out also suits his business.

**Alix Pasquet III** is Managing Partner of Prime Macaya Capital Management and came to investing from backgammon and poker. The show notes for the likely-matching episode list three sources of advantage: **analytical, informational and behavioural**. Pull-quotes include "Markets are not about individuals, they're about groups. If you're going to have a behavioral advantage, it's about exploiting group behavior." He reduces game theory to "Play weaker players. Don't get into the ring with Mike Tyson." He also says "You have a competitive advantage at being yourself" ([Podscan](https://podscan.fm/podcasts/odds-on-open/episodes/hedge-fund-manager-alix-pasquet-how-small-funds-can-outsmart-multi-managers)). For small players he recommends field research and competitive-strategy work that large pods will not do. He points to "customer–investor mismatches", where investors misjudge business models they have never used as customers. He treats technical analysis as a *behavioural* tool rather than a forecast, and he prefers buying strength and new highs to bottom-fishing. He is openly sceptical of AI: "Over-reliance on AI tools is going to create the worst portfolio managers that we've ever seen a decade from now". Instead he recommends a "bionic" investor who guards five skills: pattern recall, visualisation, reading between the lines, leaps of judgment and synthesis (same source). In *earlier Odds on Open episodes* he says AI optimises for "speed-to-consensus", which works against alpha ([Jan 2026](https://ethankho.substack.com/p/ai-makes-analysts-lazy-hedge-fund-543)). He names overconfidence, overfitting, complexity bias and over-trust in backtests as the ways smart people lose ([Nov 2025, host summary](https://ethankho.substack.com/p/hedge-fund-manager-alix-pasquet-why-4a5)). In *2023 interviews* he credits Munger with the game-selection idea ("Competency is a relative thing") ([Pari Passu](https://restructuringnewsletter.com/p/how-to-be-great-hedge-fund-analyst)). His exact current policy on AI inside his own fund is unclear: the January notes say he keeps it out, while the September notes favour a human-plus-AI approach.

The two guests disagree about AI, and the disagreement is useful. McKeown expects AI to give everyone *equal access to data*. Pasquet warns that the same tools pull everyone *toward the same conclusions*. If both are right, a retail trader who asks an LLM the same question as everyone else, over the same data, gets consensus, and consensus earns no alpha. What remains is **the question you ask, the domain priors you bring, the arena you choose, and the discipline you keep**. Neither set of show notes gives concrete sizing rules or advice aimed at retail traders. The guidance is written for small funds and junior analysts, and applying it to an individual trader is my synthesis.

## A UK individual's real edges are structural, not informational

Multi-manager platforms show where retail cannot compete. According to a practitioner write-up (indicative, not primary filings), Millennium runs **330+ pods**, typically with $100–200m each. It reportedly halves a pod's capital at a **5% drawdown** and shuts it at **7.5%**, under tight factor and crowding limits, with capital reallocated daily ([Young and Calculated](https://youngandcalculated.substack.com/p/how-multi-manager-hedge-funds-actually)). That machine is built to extract short-horizon, factor-neutral, catalyst-driven alpha from liquid names, using data, analysts and execution that retail cannot match. Trading earnings surprises or index CFDs intraday means stepping into their ring.

An individual has what pods lack. There is no career or benchmark risk and no redemptions. The time horizon is unlimited, which means you can sit through multi-year factor droughts that institutions give up on. Small size lets you hold microcaps and UK AIM names that billion-dollar funds cannot, and those segments have thinner coverage and wider factor spreads ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/microcaps-are-beating-large-caps-allocators-arent-biting); [AAII](https://www.aaii.com/model-portfolios/theadvantagesmicrocapstocks)). Most microcap advocacy comes from managers with a commercial interest, and I found no independent study of the edge *after* the higher costs retail pays. You also have ISA and SIPP wrappers, and the option of not trading at all. Low-turnover investors in low-volatility US stock funds captured about **97%** of fund returns ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap)). Institutional statistical hygiene is also cheap to adopt: Sharpe ratios deflated for the number of trials tested, and genuine out-of-sample tests ([Deflated Sharpe](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551)).

Ranked by strength of evidence, the realistic sources of return for an individual are:

1. Cheap market exposure held for a long time inside an ISA or SIPP, while avoiding the behaviour gap.
2. Systematic factor tilts (value, momentum, quality, size) held through the droughts that institutions abandon.
3. Capacity-constrained niches where a £10k–£1m position is feasible.
4. Real domain expertise applied over months and years. This is Pasquet's customer–investor mismatch and McKeown's "domain context plus data".

Short-horizon directional trading in liquid FX, index CFDs and 0DTE options is where the evidence against retail is strongest.

UK tax changes the picture. Spread-bet profits are generally free of CGT and income tax because they count as betting, but **losses cannot be offset**. CFD gains are subject to CGT at **18%/24%** in 2026/27 with a **£3,000** annual exempt amount, and losses can be carried forward. None of these products can go in an ISA or SIPP ([Stewart Accounting](https://stewartaccounting.co.uk/spread-betting-cfds-and-forex-how-hmrc-taxes-each-one-in-2026-27/); [IG](https://www.ig.com/uk/learn/trading/spread-betting-vs-cfds-tax)). These statements rely on accountant and broker sources, not HMRC manuals. The practical point is that a tax exemption on gains is only worth something if you win, and the base rate says most people lose. Frequent automated trading could also strengthen an HMRC argument that the activity is a trade, taxable as income. Retail access to crypto ETNs opened on **8 October 2025**, with no FSCS cover, and crypto derivatives remain banned for retail ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-opens-retail-access-crypto-etns)).

## Commodities offer premia, not an easier hunting ground

Commodities have real, documented return sources. Fully collateralised commodity futures earned a risk premium comparable to equities over 1959–2014, with a **Sharpe ratio of 0.39 against 0.40 for equities** and low correlation outside crises. A long high-basis / short low-basis (carry) strategy returned about **9.4% a year, both in-sample and out-of-sample** ([Gorton, Rouwenhorst & Bhardwaj, NBER w21243](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21243/w21243.pdf)). Trend following was profitable in every decade from 1880 in AQR's century-long study ([Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing)), and basis-momentum adds predictive power beyond carry and momentum ([Boons & Prado, JF 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.12738)). The basis signal is at bottom an inventory signal, which fits McKeown's point about inventory driving boom-bust cycles.

The same evidence shows why commodities are harder for retail, not easier. First, the playing field is tilted:

- **Opponents are the physical market.** Trafigura made **$4.1bn net profit in six months** to March 2026, more than its whole previous year, and its CEO attributed that to logistics complexity rather than prices ([Kitco/Reuters](https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2026-06-04/trafigura-profit-volumes-surge-first-results-hormuz-closure)).
- **The systematic premia are crowded.** CTAs run trend and carry at scale.
- **The premia are cyclical.** The SG Trend Index made **+27.4% in 2022**, then suffered a **−20.4% drawdown** from May 2024 to May 2025, its second-worst since 2000 ([Cambridge Associates](https://www.cambridgeassociates.com/insight/does-trend-followings-recent-struggle-signal-that-the-strategy-is-structurally-broken/)). It then made **+2.4%** in 2025, against **+17.9%** for the S&P 500 ([Top Traders Unplugged](https://www.toptradersunplugged.com/trend-following-performance-report-december-2025/)).

Second, the instruments carry costs and tail risks that equities do not:

- **Roll costs.** Holding futures is not the same as holding the commodity. USO returned about **55–57%** over a decade in which spot WTI roughly tripled from its low ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/05/26/usos-front-month-oil-strategy-has-lagged-crude-oil-itself-by-half-since-2014-and-the-roll-cost-is-the-reason/)).
- **Negative prices.** WTI went below zero in April 2020, while retail money poured into a fund that no longer tracked the oil price ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/04/23/young-investors-rush-into-struggling-oil-etf-that-isnt-even-tracking-the-price-of-oil-anymore.html)).
- **Rule changes mid-crisis.** The LME cancelled about **$12bn of nickel trades** in 2022 ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/business/banking-finance/article/3226885/lmes-nickel-crisis-decision-heart-dispute-uk-judicial-review-involving-us12-billion-cancelled-trades)).

The 2026 Iran war shows how these conditions play out. After the Strait of Hormuz closed in March, Brent reportedly went from **$72 to $118, back to $72 and then to about $100 within five months** ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_impact_of_the_2026_Iran_war)). Reuters gives somewhat different peak figures ([Kitco/Reuters](https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2026-06-04/trafigura-profit-volumes-surge-first-results-hormuz-closure)). Swings like that hurt some trend funds ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/ctas-racked-big-gains-least-iran-war-upended-markets)). They also trigger the 50% margin close-out on leveraged retail CFDs. How CTAs performed after March 2026 is **unverified**.

My conclusion is that commodities work for a UK retail trader as an **allocation or a systematic sleeve**: diversified trend and carry, either through a managed-futures fund or through CME micro futures such as Micro WTI at 100 barrels ([CME](https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/energy/crude-oil/micro-wti-crude-oil.contractSpecs.html)). They do not work as a hunting ground for discretionary informational alpha. Micro futures give honest exchange pricing and direct exposure to the term structure, but futures accounts have no negative balance protection. The academic evidence for trend following assumes 15–30+ markets, and a small account cannot diversify that widely, so its results will be lumpier than the backtests. Any genuine discretionary edge in commodities is likely to come from domain knowledge (for example, you work in agriculture, shipping or energy), applied slowly and at small size.

## The evidence favours AI as research engine, not oracle

Machine learning finds real predictability in returns, but only a little. Gu, Kelly and Xiu report a monthly out-of-sample **R² of about 0.4%**, driven by familiar signals (momentum, liquidity, volatility) and concentrated in small, illiquid stocks ([Gu, Kelly & Xiu](https://dachxiu.chicagobooth.edu/download/ML.pdf)). LLM news sentiment worked impressively right after ChatGPT appeared, but its **Sharpe fell from 6.54 to 1.22 by mid-2024**, and the strategy becomes **unprofitable above about 20bp in costs**, a level retail traders easily exceed in the small caps where the signal is strongest ([Lopez-Lira & Tang, JFE 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). Much apparent LLM skill in small caps turns out to be **memorised training data**: look-ahead propensity explains about 37% of the measured effect, and the effect disappears after the model's training cutoff ([Gao, Jiang & Yan](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)). When 14 frontier models traded Dow stocks after their cutoffs, the best made about 2%, and **"most LLM agents fail to outperform"** buy-and-hold ([StockBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2)). In a live crypto contest, results ranged from **+22% to −63%**, and commentators blamed overtrading and leverage. That is the retail failure pattern, automated ([iWeaver summary of Alpha Arena S1](https://www.iweaver.ai/blog/alpha-arena-ai-trading-season-1-results/)).

For a solo builder the biggest danger is overfitting, and AI coding assistants make it worse, because they let you try thousands of strategy variants almost for free. With five years of data, testing about **45 configurations** is enough to expect an in-sample Sharpe of 1 from pure noise. A search over 8,800 variants on a *random walk* produced a Sharpe of 1.27 ([Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-pseudo.pdf)). For overfit strategies, in-sample and out-of-sample performance are *negatively* correlated ([Probability of Backtest Overfitting](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-prob.pdf)). Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue that a new factor needs a t-statistic above about **3.0**, not 2.0 ([summary](https://foxholm.com/q/research/harvey-liu-zhu-cross-section/)). López de Prado's toolkit is the standard defence: purged and embargoed cross-validation, triple-barrier labelling and meta-labelling ([AFML](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Advances+in+Financial+Machine+Learning-p-9781119482086)).

These results point to one design. The LLM sits in the *research and engineering layer*, where it:

- turns filings, RNS announcements and earnings-call Q&A into structured features;
- writes and unit-tests data pipelines;
- checks code for look-ahead bugs;
- drafts risk reports;
- runs the post-trade journal.

Pasquet's "bionic" framing fits here, with the human keeping the question and the final synthesis. Extracting features from text holds up better than asking a model for a direct return call: look-ahead bias explained only about 19% of an LLM-extracted capex signal ([Gao, Jiang & Yan](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)). The *decision and risk layer* stays conventional and deterministic: volatility targeting, at most half-Kelly sizing, hard loss and exposure limits, and a kill switch that runs outside the model. Meta-labelling is a natural bridge. A rule or human idea generates the signal, and ML only decides whether to take it and how large.

A practical UK stack looks like this:

Selling signals, running a copy-trading group or managing family money probably would require it.

## Action plan

The phases and timings below are my suggested sequence, built from the evidence above. They are not taken from either guest.

### Phased roadmap for an AI-assisted system

| Phase | Timing | What to do | Gate to move on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Foundations | Weeks 0–4 | Put core savings in low-cost diversified holdings inside an ISA or SIPP, kept separate from a trading "risk budget" you can afford to lose entirely. Write down your arena (Pasquet's game selection): which market, which holding period, and why you have an edge there over the likely counterparty. Start a decision journal for every idea, even ones you don't trade. | A one-page thesis naming the arena, the edge source (behavioural, structural or domain), the expected counterparty, and what would prove you wrong |
| 1. Research infrastructure | Months 1–3 | Get point-in-time, survivorship-free data. Set up vectorbt for exploration and LEAN or Nautilus for the live code path. Keep an **append-only trial log** that records every variant, including the ones the AI generated. Pin model versions and log prompts and outputs. Use the LLM to write and test code, not to pick trades. | A data pipeline with tests, plus a trial log running automatically |
| 2. Hypothesis and validation | Months 3–6 | Pre-register 3–5 hypotheses, each with an economic reason (for example a behavioural tilt, carry, or a feature extracted from text). Use walk-forward or purged, embargoed cross-validation plus a final holdout you never touch. Deflate the Sharpe by your trial count and estimate PBO. Model costs pessimistically, at spread plus slippage plus financing. Test LLM-derived signals **only on data after the model's training cutoff**, or with names and dates masked. | Deflated Sharpe still positive, PBO low, survives doubled costs, t-stat around 3 or higher |
| 3. Paper trading and risk layer | Months 6–9 | Build deterministic risk controls outside the LLM: a volatility target, at most half-Kelly sizing, daily and weekly loss limits, per-instrument caps, idempotent orders, reconciliation against the broker, and an independent kill switch. Run a broker demo or paper account. | Paper results within the expected range, with no operational incidents for 8–12 weeks |
| 4. Small live capital | Months 9–18 | Trade live at a fraction of intended size. Expect live performance to be noticeably worse than the backtest (my inference from the overfitting and decay evidence). Pre-set an abandon rule, for example underperformance below a threshold after N trades. Review the journal every quarter, measure hit rate and payoff ratio (McKeown's "sample size beats anecdotes"), and prune. | Only scale up after a statistically meaningful live sample |

### Checklist of retail mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Evidence | Your countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Overtrading | Highest-turnover households lagged by about 80bp a month ([Barber & Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/individual_investor_performance_final.pdf)) | Set a holding-period floor and a trade budget, and log the cost of every trade |
| Short-dated lottery products | 0DTE lost 4.6% per trade ([Bogousslavsky & Muravyev](https://www.lsu.edu/business/files/event-files/2025-finance-mardi-gras/retail_option_trading_v2.pdf)) | Avoid 0DTE and near-expiry contracts, and use limit orders |
| Excess leverage | 74–89% of CFD accounts lost money before the leverage caps ([ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/press-news/esma-news/esma-agrees-prohibit-binary-options-and-restrict-cfds-protect-retail-investors)) | Use a volatility target, and do not opt up to professional status just to get more leverage |
| Following the crowd | −4.7% abnormal returns on Robinhood's most-bought stocks ([Barber et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3715077)) | Ban trades sourced from social media or finfluencers. Pasquet's point is to exploit group behaviour, not join it |
| Refusing to quit when losing | 95.3% of experienced losers kept trading ([Barber et al.](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/Day%20Traders/Day%20Trading%20and%20Learning%20110217.pdf)) | Pre-commit to an abandon rule in writing |
| Overfit backtests | Testing about 45 variants gives a Sharpe of 1 from noise ([Bailey et al.](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-pseudo.pdf)) | Keep the trial log and use deflated Sharpe, PBO and a holdout |
| Trusting an LLM's hindsight | Memorisation explains much of LLM predictability in small caps ([Gao, Jiang & Yan](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)) | Test only after the model's cutoff, mask inputs, and check facts against your own data |
| Letting AI make and size trades | Agents failed to beat buy-and-hold ([StockBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2)) | Keep the LLM in the research layer and hard-code risk |
| Ignoring roll costs | USO lagged spot WTI badly ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/05/26/usos-front-month-oil-strategy-has-lagged-crude-oil-itself-by-half-since-2014-and-the-roll-cost-is-the-reason/)) | Model the term structure before holding any commodity product |

### Deciding whether and how to approach commodities

Work through five questions in order:

1. **Do you want diversification, or do you want alpha?** If you want diversification, a managed-futures fund or UCITS sleeve at 5–15% of the risk budget is simpler than building your own system. That range is my illustration, not a recommendation.
2. **Can you fund 10 or more uncorrelated markets through micro futures, and survive a −20% drawdown lasting a year?** If not, a self-run trend or carry book will be too concentrated to resemble the evidence.
3. **Do you have real domain knowledge in a specific commodity chain?** Only then consider discretionary trades, with long horizons, small size, and no near-expiry or illiquid contracts.
4. **Can you tolerate futures losses larger than your account?** If not, prefer funds or ETCs, but check how each one rolls its contracts.
5. **Have you tested carry, trend or basis-momentum through the Phase 2 gates?** If not, do not trade them live.

In every case, treat an event like Hormuz as normal for this asset class, not as an exception, and size positions so that a $72→$118→$72 round trip cannot trigger a margin close-out.

## Conclusion

The advice from both guests amounts to a kind of reverse engineering. Work out where the institutions *cannot* go, whether because of capacity, horizon, benchmark or career constraints, and do your thinking there. Much of what gets called retail "alpha" is really the avoidance of negative alpha. Cutting turnover, leverage and short-dated products recovers most of the gap between the typical retail trader and the market before any clever signal is needed. AI changes the economics of *research*. It does not change the economics of *prediction*. The trader who benefits is the one who uses it to test more honestly, log every trial and enforce their own rules, not the one who uses it to generate more trades.

One tension deserves attention as the system gets built. McKeown's world of cheap, AI-accessible data and Pasquet's warning about speed-to-consensus are both likely to prove right at once. As more retail traders plug the same models into the same data feeds, LLM-derived signals will crowd and decay faster than the 2021–24 sentiment decline already recorded. A durable edge for a UK individual is therefore likely to come from the parts of the process an LLM cannot supply: the arena you choose, the specialised knowledge you bring to it, and a record of how you actually traded. *Not financial advice.*
