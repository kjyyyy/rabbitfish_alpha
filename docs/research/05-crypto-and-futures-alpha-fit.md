# Your gates transfer; your search engine does not

*Not financial advice. This is an educational synthesis of published research and documentation. It recommends no trade, venue, broker, wallet or tax structure, and nothing here should be acted on without independent professional advice.*

Alpha Lab's validation machinery — trial-counted Deflated Sharpe, PBO, purged holdouts, the leakage audit, the hash-stamped pre-registration ledger — is the single most transferable thing you own, and it transfers to crypto and commodity futures essentially unchanged. Your *discovery* machinery does not. The fundamental law of active management makes breadth the multiplier on information coefficient, and the investable universe collapses from a 3,000-name A-share cross-section to roughly 25 liquid commodity contracts carrying an estimated **four to eight independent bets**, or 100–200 liquid crypto perpetuals whose documented premia sit in exactly the coins where prices are least trustworthy. A genetic-programming refiner and a Thompson-sampling bandit searching thousands of expressions over a five-bet universe is a machine for manufacturing the false positives your own gates are built to catch — which is presumably why zero factors have ever passed a fully-trial-counted Deflated Sharpe test, and why moving asset class will not change that. The strategic answer that falls out of the evidence is uncomfortable but clean: **commodity futures is the better destination and crypto is the better laboratory**. Commodity carry paid roughly 10% a year in both the 1959–2004 and 2005–2014 samples ([Bhardwaj, Gorton & Rouwenhorst, NBER w21243](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21243/w21243.pdf)) while crypto's flagship mechanism — perpetual funding — has collapsed to a formula floor of about 11% APR that prints as a *constant* 78% of the time on BTC ([BitMEX Q3 2025 Derivatives Report](https://www.bitmex.com/blog/2025q3-derivatives-report)) and is barred to UK retail by the FCA's still-standing crypto-derivatives ban ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-bans-sale-crypto-derivatives-retail-consumers)). But commodity per-contract data is not free — Nasdaq Data Link's CHRIS continuous-futures database is dead with no replacement ([Packt issue #5](https://github.com/PacktPublishing/Python-for-Algorithmic-Trading-Cookbook/issues/5)) — whereas Binance publishes bulk history for individual dated COIN-M quarterly contracts for nothing ([binance-public-data](https://github.com/binance/binance-public-data)). So you build and prove the roll engine on free crypto dated futures, then port it. And you build no execution path at all until something survives forward pre-registration.

## The gates are institutional-grade; the search engine is fighting the wrong arithmetic

Set against the documented institutional pipeline, Alpha Lab is strong in an unusual place. The staged funnel a systematic fund runs — hypothesis, feature construction, in-sample evaluation, out-of-sample validation, production-readiness screens for capacity and decay and correlation-to-book, regime testing, paper trading, staged capital, live monitoring, decommissioning — is described in secondary practitioner synthesis rather than any fund's own account ([Young & Calculated](https://youngandcalculated.substack.com/p/how-quant-hedge-funds-actually-build)), and the underlying research notes flag that no primary, attributable source describes the *governance* stages: who sits on a research committee, what documentation a signal carries into review, how capital votes happen. That matters, because it means the part of the institution you cannot copy is less documented than the part you can. What is documented is the statistics, and you have it. López de Prado's account of the dominant failure mode — "research through backtesting," repeatedly testing on the same data until a favourable result appears, which he characterises citing the American Statistical Association as "scientific fraud," and which takes **only about 20 iterations to manufacture a false strategy at standard significance** ([GARP whitepaper](https://www.garp.org/hubfs/Whitepapers/a1Z1W0000054x6lUAA.pdf)) — is precisely what your append-only trial ledger and cumulative-trial-count Deflated Sharpe exist to prevent. The arithmetic behind DSR, that testing *I* independent strategies on pure noise yields an expected maximum Sharpe of roughly **√(2 log I)**, so 100 trials produce a best-of-set Sharpe of about 2.1 from nothing ([Bailey & López de Prado](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551); [GARP](https://www.garp.org/hubfs/Whitepapers/a1Z1W0000054x6lUAA.pdf)), is the arithmetic your gates already run. Harvey, Liu and Zhu's argument that **the minimum t-statistic for a new factor should be 3.0, not 2.0** ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2249314)) is already your bar.

The critical point is that these statistics are downstream of an honest trial count, and the trial count is unobservable to anyone but the researcher. Institutions solve this by instrumenting the research platform so every run is logged automatically — turning a cultural control into an infrastructural one. Your ledger does that. On the specific question of how many times a locked holdout may be touched, and on the specific drawdown or IC thresholds at which a live strategy is switched off, **the research found no citable institutional policy at any named fund**; these appear to be folklore rather than documented practice, and Maven Securities, who publish on decay, explicitly give no kill thresholds and prescribe perpetual adaptation instead ([Maven Securities](https://www.mavensecurities.com/alpha-decay-what-does-it-look-like-and-what-does-it-mean-for-systematic-traders/)). Treat any number you have seen quoted for either as unsourced. The corollary is genuinely favourable to you: since institutional kill decisions appear to be committee judgement calls against a prior, a solo lab that pre-commits an automated kill rule is running *tighter* discipline than the institution, not looser.

Four things are missing that matter, and all four are cheap. First, **walk-forward validation is itself criticised as overfittable**: it tests a single historical path, results depend heavily on the particular sequence, a strategy may excel forward and fail backward, and early decisions use minimal data. The prescribed replacement is combinatorial purged cross-validation, generating thousands of train/test combinations so every part of the series is tested many times, yielding a *distribution* of Sharpe ratios rather than one likely-overfit number ([GARP](https://www.garp.org/hubfs/Whitepapers/a1Z1W0000054x6lUAA.pdf)). CPCV is compute-bound, not headcount-bound; it is the highest-leverage institutional technique available to you and you currently have the weaker cousin. Second, the **Overfitting Factor**. Rej, Seager and Bouchaud at Capital Fund Management model the iterative-refinement process itself rather than applying Bonferroni, defining OFF = E[SR in-sample] / E[SR out-of-sample], and for typical CTA strategies with Sharpe 0.3–0.5 find "the discounting factor is ≃ 2" — **halve the reported backtest Sharpe** ([arXiv 1902.01802](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1902.01802)). Their key parameters are *f*, the fraction of P&L sections the researcher modifies, typically capped around 0.1, and Θ, the acceptance threshold, typically about 0.7 Sharpe. The selection-bias channel they identify is the one your bandit is structurally most exposed to: strategies that *fail* the threshold get tweaked until they pass, and the tweaking is where the bias enters. OFF is more useful to you than Bonferroni or even DSR, because it is calibrated to how iterative refinement actually proceeds rather than to independent parallel trials, and it needs no trial count at all. Combining Θ ≈ 0.7 with OFF ≈ 2 gives a directly usable rule: **a candidate needs a backtest Sharpe near 1.4 before it is a credible 0.7 live.** Third, a **correlation-to-existing-book gate** before any new signal enters the library — an explicit institutional production screen, cheap to replicate, and the thing that caps the marginal contribution of the twentieth variant of a signal you already own. Fourth, **Maven's lagged-signal test**: re-run the strategy with deliberately delayed signals and measure the annualised return difference ([Maven Securities](https://www.mavensecurities.com/alpha-decay-what-does-it-look-like-and-what-does-it-mean-for-systematic-traders/)). It needs no extra data and answers three questions at once — how urgent is execution, how much slippage tolerance exists, is the edge decaying. Maven measure the average cost of delay at **9.9% in Europe and 5.6% in the US, rising at 36 bps/year in the US and 16 bps/year in Europe**, which is also the cleanest available quantification of the speed disadvantage you cannot fix.

Now the part that is more interesting than the gap list: a substantial amount of institutional machinery you should deliberately *not* build. The **factor alignment problem** is the strongest example. When alpha signals contain components not spanned by the risk model, optimised active portfolios systematically underestimate the risk of exactly the positions the optimiser most wants to take; the vendor remedy is the Alpha Alignment Factor, augmenting the risk model with a factor spanning the orthogonal component of alpha ([Saxena & Stubbs](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259384627_The_Alpha_Alignment_Factor_A_Solution_to_the_Underestimation_of_Risk_for_Optimized_Active_Portfolios)). A related documented effect is that risk-model factors can absorb alpha outright — the choice of risk model mechanically determines how much signal survives neutralisation ([Saxena & Stubbs, "Do Risk Factors Eat Alphas?"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/242584040_Do_Risk_Factors_Eat_Alphas)). This is simultaneously the strongest argument for owning a commercial risk model and the strongest argument that, lacking one, you should **avoid heavy mean-variance optimisation entirely**. An optimiser converts risk-model error into position error with leverage. A solo lab running an optimiser on a statistical PCA covariance is in the worst available configuration: maximum sensitivity to the least reliable input. Equal-weight, IC-weight and shrunk or hierarchical schemes are robustness-dominant at your scale. Your price-only risk model earns its keep as a *diagnostic* — it is what told you 62% of your best signal was style — not as an optimiser input. Likewise, feature stores priced at **$50,000–$500,000 annually** ([Quant 2.0 Architecture](https://altstreet.investments/blog/quant-2-architecture-modern-trading-stack-ai-mlops), a consultancy blog rather than a fund engineering account) solve a multi-team coordination problem that does not exist at n=1; the one genuine problem they address, train/serve skew, you solve by computing features through a single code path. And point-in-time licensed fundamentals and corporate actions — the one stack layer with no free equivalent, and where institutional money genuinely buys advantage — are an *equities* problem. Futures and crypto have almost no point-in-time data moat: prices, volumes, open interest, funding and basis are cheaply available and rarely restated. That is a strong structural argument for moving markets, independent of any signal.

The cost evidence sharpens this further, and cuts in a way most retail writing gets backwards. AQR's Frazzini, Israel and Moskowitz analysed **$1.7 trillion of live executed trades across 21 developed equity markets from August 1998 to June 2016 — roughly 691 million executions across 9,543 stocks** — and measured **mean market impact of 9.97 bps, median 6.18 bps, value-weighted 15.14 bps**, with a formal test confirming a power-law functional form with **coefficient close to ½**, and with trade size as a fraction of daily volume the dominant driver ([AQR Trading Costs](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2021/08/Trading-Cost.pdf)). Their most under-appreciated finding is that TAQ-calibrated academic cost models produce estimates "several times larger" than realised costs, because aggregated public data mixes informed insiders, retail and impatient liquidity demanders, masking what patient limit-order execution actually achieves ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsxq9yadiccew11t8idc/portfolio/aqr-investor-worries-over-trading-costs-may-be-overblown)). Two consequences follow for you. Because your participation rate is effectively zero, the square-root impact term is negligible and your cost is **spread plus fees plus funding — known constants, not model outputs**. Solo cost modelling is therefore *more* reliable than institutional cost modelling, not less, which is the opposite of the usual assumption and is exactly why your leakage audit's finding that **11 percentage points a year of apparent alpha was evaluation convention** is so damning: with impact near zero, the residual failure mode is almost purely statistical self-deception. And because the same square-root law mechanically caps capacity, the institutional production screen "maximum AUM at which the signal retains positive net returns" throws away precisely the opportunity set you should target. **Treat "capacity too small for a fund" as a positive screen.** That single inversion is the most exploitable asymmetry in the entire institutional pipeline, and it survives the move to crypto and commodities intact.

Where it does not survive is breadth. IR = IC · √BR means a mediocre signal across 500 names beats a strong signal on ten. With roughly 25 liquid commodity contracts and an inferred 4–8 independent bets, or 100–200 liquid crypto perps whose cross-sectional premia concentrate in illiquid names, you cannot use breadth to rescue weak ICs. You need higher-IC, lower-capacity signals — the uncontested segment — and you need a *narrower* search, because trial count is the numerator of your own deflation. The lab's architecture currently optimises for the opposite.

## Crypto's flagship mechanism pays 11% at a formula floor you cannot legally reach

The economically robust story in crypto is singular: **leverage demand**. Retail and quasi-retail longs pay to be long with leverage, and that payment surfaces as perpetual funding, as futures basis, and as the options volatility premium. Everything else — cross-sectional momentum, reversal, size — is a statistical regularity whose net-of-cost survival is far less certain. The problem is that the leverage-demand trade has been arbitraged down to its structural minimum. The standard perpetual funding formula contains a fixed interest-rate component of **0.01% per 8 hours, roughly 11–11.6% APR**, and funding stays pinned at exactly that whenever the average premium index sits between −0.04% and +0.06% ([Hyperliquid Docs](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/trading/funding); [BitMEX Q3 2025](https://www.bitmex.com/blog/2025q3-derivatives-report)). Where BTC funding "frequently exceeded 0.2% per 8 hours during bull markets, translating to an annualised rate of over 200%" in the early perpetual years, the current cycle has established "a new normal" at that 0.01% baseline. In Q3 2025, realised mean 8-hour BTC funding was **0.0081% on BitMEX, 0.0057% on Binance and 0.0120% on Hyperliquid** — the Binance figure is about **6.2% annualised gross, before fees** — and BitMEX BTC printed exactly the floor **78.19% of the quarter**, ETH **87.52%** ([BitMEX Q3 2025](https://www.bitmex.com/blog/2025q3-derivatives-report)). Most of the time the "signal" is a constant. The compression mechanism is visible: Ethena's USDe alone held **$7.83 billion ready to deploy against roughly $65.70 billion of combined BTC/ETH/SOL open interest** as of 21 September 2025. Delta-neutral synthetic-dollar products that "ran as high as 20% to 30%" in 2023–24 were paying "closer to 3.6%" by mid-2026 ([Investor's Handbook](https://medium.com/the-investors-handbook/delta-neutral-yield-explained-how-basis-trade-synthetic-dollars-really-work-and-where-they-2db6bd446e22)), and CME bitcoin futures activity slumped to a 14-month low as the institutional basis trade unwound ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/396722/cme-bitcoin-futures-activity-slumps-to-14-month-low-as-basis-trade-unwind-drains-institutional-demand)).

The academic decay picture is consistent. Perpetual-versus-spot mean absolute deviations ran at **60–100% annualised in 2020–2021** and "narrow considerably in 2022, suggesting improving market efficiency," with BTC arbitrage Sharpe under high trading-cost assumptions falling from **2.39 in 2021 to 1.23 in 2022** ([He, Manela, Ross & von Sydow, arXiv 2212.06888](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06888v2)). A six-month academic backtest of funding-rate arbitrage (August 2023 – February 2024) found CEX BTC strategies returned only **1.97–2.17% over six months with negative Sharpe ratios of −7.34 on Binance and −7.93 on BitMEX**, versus 5.57–7.61% on DEXs ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2096720925000818)). That study's headline case — **Drift XRP at 7x leverage returning 115.9% over six months with 1.92% max drawdown and Sharpe 15.85** — should be read as an upper bound and a warning, not a base case: it is a leveraged position on a small venue over a calm window, and the authors themselves caution that "findings might not be applicable in future scenarios as markets evolve."

Momentum is the one return-predictive family with credible cost-aware evidence, and it is time-series momentum rather than cross-sectional. The canonical cross-sectional result — Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu's weekly long-short returns of **2.7% (1-week, t=1.99), 3.3% (2-week, t=2.44), 4.1% (3-week, t=2.74) and 2.5% (4-week, t=2.00)** across 1,707 coins from 2014–2018 ([NBER w25882](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25882/w25882.pdf)) — carries the authors' own caveat that results "do not take into account trading costs and the feasibility of short selling," and that "short selling is either not possible or limited for most of the coins." The later, cost-aware study running to August 2023 found the best cross-sectional configuration (14-day formation, 7-day hold) reached **Sharpe 1.28 versus a market Sharpe of 1.01**, but that **5 of 21 cross-sectional portfolios were liquidated during the sample** and only 6 of 21 beat the market; the optimal time-series configuration (28-day lookback, 5-day hold) reached **Sharpe 1.51 versus market 0.85 with max drawdown 61.8% versus 89.1%**, and time-series momentum "survives costs with minimal degradation" at 15 bps per trade while cross-sectional strategies "show more significant erosion" ([AUT ACFR](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/918729/Time_Series_and_Cross_Sectional_Momentum_in_the_Cryptocurrency_Market_with_IA.pdf)). Note what that Sharpe is made of: trend earns it mostly by *avoiding* drawdowns, not by adding return. Two smaller families deserve mention. The **bitcoin variance risk premium runs about 14% annualised at the one-month horizon versus roughly 2% for the S&P 500** — about seven times the equity VRP — but is highest in low-volatility regimes where the tail is fattest, which is the structural warning for any short-vol book ([arXiv 2410.15195](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15195v2)). And token unlocks are a genuinely small-book-shaped event edge: across 236 events, one-month median return was **−16.26% versus BTC**, the tradable window is **pre-event (one-month pre-unlock median −14.7%, p<0.001)**, and the effect exists **only in thin-float names (−16.02% median, n=77, p=0.03) and is statistically absent in established high-liquidity tokens (−2.57%, p=0.42)** ([Unlocks.app](https://insights.unlocks.app/do-token-unlocks-crash-prices/)). A practitioner blog reporting combined Sharpe near 2.5 for orthogonal cross-sectional and funding-carry portfolios ([Aperiodic](https://blog.aperiodic.io/p/cross-sectional-alpha-factors-in)) discloses no fees, capacity or live results and should not be weighted; likewise a 2026 preprint reporting **Sharpe 2.41** for 6-hour trend across 150+ pairs ([arXiv 2602.11708](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11708)) is unrefereed with unstated cost assumptions.

Costs are the second filter and they are unforgiving at your rebalance frequency. Base-tier perpetual fees run about **0.02% maker / 0.05% taker on Binance, OKX, Bybit and Kraken**, and **0.015% / 0.045% on Hyperliquid** ([Spoted Crypto 2026](https://www.spotedcrypto.com/crypto-exchange-fees-comparison-2026-maker-taker/); [Hyperliquid Guide](https://hyperliquidguide.com/guides/fees/fees-explained) — a secondary source, treat as indicative). Spot is worse: **Kraken 0.25%/0.40% and Coinbase Advanced 0.40%/0.60%**, which at roughly 120 bps per round trip is disqualifying for any mid-frequency spot leg. The defensible planning figure is the AUT study's **15 bps all-in per trade on liquid Binance pairs, decomposed as ~10 bps fees + 3.26 bps tick size + 1.53 bps average slippage** — and that slippage figure is a liquid-universe average that will understate costs badly below the top ~50 coins. Do the arithmetic that matters: **at 15 bps per trade, a weekly-rebalanced cross-sectional book with 50% one-way turnover pays roughly 15 bps a week, about 7.8% a year.** Against the 4.1%/week gross spread measured in 2014–2018 that is trivial; against anything resembling current spreads on liquid coins it is decisive. Maker-only execution roughly halves-to-quarters the fee bill, and at 4-hour-to-2-week horizons there is no reason to cross the spread on entry, so **a passive execution layer is worth more than most signal improvements** — which is the one place the AQR patience finding genuinely transfers to a small book.

The methodological traps are where your existing machinery is closest to relevant and still insufficient. Crypto breaks four assumptions equity research relies on. There is **no daily close** — funding settles 8-hourly on CEXs and hourly on Hyperliquid, so the "day" is an arbitrary researcher choice and the rebalance hour is a free parameter; reporting a Sharpe for one rebalance hour without showing the surface across all 24 is a soft form of overfitting, and a long-biased perp backtest that ignores funding overstates returns by roughly **11% annualised at the floor** (funding was positive over 92% of Q3 2025). There is **no survivorship-free index membership**; universes are built by retrospectively filtering aggregator data, and universe definition is a first-order researcher degree of freedom. **Reported volume is substantially fabricated**: wash trading averaged **77.5% of reported volume on unregulated exchanges, median 79.1% — 53.4% on Tier-1 and 81.7% on Tier-2 unregulated venues — versus under 5% on regulated exchanges**, implying over $4.5 trillion of fake spot volume in Q1 2020 alone, detected via Benford's Law, trade-size clustering and power-law tail tests across 448,475,535 transactions ([Cong, Li, Tang & Yang, NBER w30783](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30783/w30783.pdf)). A volume-based liquidity screen in crypto is therefore partly a wash-trading screen with the sign unknown; screen on order-book depth or on regulated-venue volume instead. That estimate is from 2019 data and **no comparable rigorous update for 2024–2026 was found**, so whether wash trading fell with venue concentration or migrated to perp DEXs is unknown. And **fat tails break standard inference**: under jump-diffusion, "conventional t-tests on mean returns are insufficient to test profitability," and portfolios can show statistically significant positive *mean* returns while producing negative cumulative *log* returns ([AUT ACFR](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/918729/Time_Series_and_Cross_Sectional_Momentum_in_the_Cryptocurrency_Market_with_IA.pdf)). This is the single most underused correction in the crypto literature and it is a direct, cheap addition to your evaluation layer: **profitability must be tested on compounded log wealth, not on a t-test of arithmetic means.** Your existing DSR gate does not catch this, because DSR deflates a Sharpe computed on the wrong statistic.

Survivorship is the trap everyone names and nobody measures. **No source quantifies the survivorship-bias uplift for crypto momentum** — every practitioner source asserts it matters and none reports a with-versus-without comparison. The most-cited write-up states that ignoring delisted 2022 projects creates "a mathematical illusion of profit" but stops short of measuring it, and separately reports that Monte Carlo resampling "exposed that the results were highly dependent on a favorable entry point rather than pure alpha" ([Pareto Quant](https://paretoquant.substack.com/p/dead-bombers-and-zombie-coins-the)). That is a genuine hole in the public record; do not let anyone fill it from memory. The subtler version is that market cap equals price times circulating supply, and **circulating supply is frequently restated retrospectively**, so a "top-50 by market cap at date t" universe built from a current API call embeds both survivorship and supply-restatement look-ahead — and no source measures the restatement component either. The defensible minimum standard is a dated point-in-time universe including delistings with a realistic delisting fill rather than last price, depth-based liquidity screening, explicit funding accrual at venue frequency, log-wealth profitability testing, at least 15 bps all-in cost, and block-bootstrap resampling of the path. Note that these corrections are multiplicative: a backtest on currently-listed coins, screened on reported volume, using arithmetic-mean t-stats, without funding, on a single path, can plausibly produce a Sharpe above 2 from a strategy with no edge whatsoever.

Then there is tail risk, which is not a backtest artefact. On **10 October 2025, over $19 billion was liquidated across 1.6 million accounts — $6.93 billion in 40 minutes and $3.21 billion in a single minute at 21:15 UTC, nine times larger than any previous single-day liquidation event**; BTC fell 14.5%, SOL briefly over 40%, roughly $350 billion of market cap evaporated against $217 billion of pre-crash open interest, **USDe depegged to $0.65 on Binance only**, and multiple exchanges suffered outages "preventing traders from managing positions or adding collateral" ([CoinGecko](https://www.coingecko.com/learn/october-10-crypto-crash-explained)). The delta-neutral synthetic-dollar complex shrank from a $15 billion peak to about $6.3 billion. Venue and counterparty risk in crypto is a position-level risk, not a background assumption, and a 7x delta-neutral book on a small venue with a 35%-depegged collateral asset would simply have ceased to exist that evening.

And the access constraint sits on top of all of it. The FCA's ban on the sale of crypto-derivatives — CFDs, options, futures and ETNs referencing unregulated cryptoassets — to retail consumers took effect **6 January 2021** ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-bans-sale-crypto-derivatives-retail-consumers)). The **ETN** portion was lifted in October 2025; the **derivatives** portion has not been, and the FCA states it will "keep our position on retail access to cryptoasset ETNs and derivatives under review" ([FCA cryptoasset regime](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/cryptoasset-regime); [FCA on ETNs](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-opens-retail-access-crypto-etns)). The Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Regulations 2026 passed Parliament on 4 February 2026, with the full regime applicable from **25 October 2027**, and cryptoasset derivatives explicitly among the components still to be progressed through further consultation. This is a prohibition on *firms selling to retail clients*, not a criminal prohibition on you, which is why offshore venues geo-block UK retail derivatives access with varying rigour — and Binance stopped accepting new UK users from **16 October 2023 at 5pm UK time** after its financial-promotions approver arrangement ended ([Binance](https://www.binance.com/en-GB/blog/all/update-on-binance-for-uk-users-937596818854026589)). **No source was found confirming the current UK-retail access position at any specific perp venue**, and it changes frequently. The practical read is blunt: the entire funding, basis and perp-momentum family — the only mechanisms with a robust economic story — is either unreachable or reachable only through venues with no FSCS protection, no Financial Ombudsman recourse and no regulatory remedy if they fail. For a small account that is a first-order risk, not a technicality.

So: does the approach fit crypto at mid-frequency? **Partly, and not in the shape the lab currently takes.** Formulaic cross-sectional expression mining is the worst-matched piece — low breadth, premia concentrated where prices are least reliable, costs that eat a 4%/week gross spread at realistic turnover, and a short leg that often does not exist. Low-turnover time-series trend on top-20 liquid perps is the one factor family that fits cleanly, and it is not something a GP refiner discovers; it is a known structure with two parameters. Pre-unlock shorts in thin-float tokens are genuinely capacity-constrained in your favour but require borrow in illiquid names. Cross-venue funding dispersion — Hyperliquid at 0.0120% against Binance at 0.0057% for BTC in Q3 2025, roughly a 6–7% annualised spread if you can hold the short on the high-funding venue — is capital-efficient and small-size-friendly, and carries the full 10 October risk. BTC/ETH cash-and-carry at 3.6–6% gross is arguably worse than T-bills after fees, collateral drag and tail risk. Stablecoin and DEX/CEX dislocation is effectively closed, because primary-market arbitrage is centralised among a small set of authorised participants ([BFI WP 2025-76](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/BFI_WP_2025-76.pdf)).

## Commodity carry has paid for fifty years, and your broker will not show you the curve

Commodities invert the crypto picture: weaker access problems, far stronger and more stable mechanisms, and a methodology burden that is almost entirely absent from your current stack. Start with the benchmark, because alpha has to be judged against it. An equally-weighted commodity futures index earned **5.23% annual risk premium with Sharpe 0.43 in-sample 1959–2004, and 3.67% with Sharpe 0.24 out-of-sample 2005–2014** — a decline the authors test and find "not significantly different from their historical in-sample averages," attributing much of it to near-zero T-bill collateral yields rather than to the futures premium ([NBER w21243](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21243/w21243.pdf)). Note that **individual** commodity futures earn approximately nothing: across 36 contracts the equally-weighted average excess return was **−0.51% (t = 0.04)**, while an equally-weighted monthly-rebalanced *portfolio* of the same contracts returned about **4.5%** — the gap is a diversification and rebalancing return, not a per-contract risk premium ([Erb & Harvey, NBER w11222](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11222/w11222.pdf)).

Against that, the factor evidence is the strongest in this entire report. **High-basis minus low-basis portfolios outperformed by approximately 10% annually, and this held in both the 1959–2004 and 2005–2014 samples**: "the link between fundamentals and expected returns has been stable over time" ([NBER w21243](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21243/w21243.pdf)). Roll returns explained **about 91% of the cross-sectional variation** in commodity returns from 1982–2004, with backwardated heating oil averaging +4.6% roll return and contangoed gold −4.9% ([Erb & Harvey](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11222/w11222.pdf)). But the same paper contains the warning that governs your horizon choice: **average spot-return standard deviation is 26.76% against roll-return standard deviation of 9.14%**, so a carry signal has a high information ratio per unit of signal while the realised P&L is swamped by spot noise at weekly horizons.

Three caveats travel with that table. The TSMOM Sharpe above 1.0 is **gross — the paper does not explicitly address transaction costs** — and it is a four-asset-class diversified figure, not a commodity-only one, which the paper does not isolate. **Hedging pressure is a genuine unresolved conflict in the literature**: Gorton, Hayashi and Rouwenhorst *reject* the Keynesian normal-backwardation hypothesis, finding that while commercial positions correlate contemporaneously with prices (R² around 10%), **lagged hedging pressure has predictive R² below 1%** ([NBER w13249](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13249/w13249.pdf)); Fernandez-Perez and co-authors, measuring hedging pressure as a **12-month moving average** of normalised commercial short positioning over September 1992 – December 2016, report a commodity long–short earning **3.71% annualised, Sharpe 0.506** across 34 commodities and remaining competitive net of 8.6 bp costs ([Fernandez-Perez et al.](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/188161/AFP-Hedging-pressure-everywhere_Adrian-Fernandez-Perez_2018-DMC.pdf)); EDHEC ranks it at **Sharpe 0.243**, between them. The plausible reconciliation is that the smoothed 12-month measure carries information a single-period lag does not, but **no source reconciles them explicitly**, so treat it as an open disagreement rather than a settled weak factor. And **no academic replication of the full commodity factor set through 2020–2026 was found** — the COVID dislocation, the 2021–22 energy and grain shock, and 2023–25 are covered by nothing verifiable. Seasonality is documented in the literature but **no verified Sharpe or return magnitude was retrievable**.

The deeper structural point is that these mechanisms are not independent. Gorton, Hayashi and Rouwenhorst make explicit that momentum and basis both work *because* they select commodities with below-average inventories — they are partly the same bet expressed differently. Koijen et al. find commodity carry loads **positively on cross-sectional momentum and negatively on value, jointly explaining about 25% of return variation**. EDHEC reduces nine tested characteristics to six priced factors. Meanwhile the universe itself clusters hard: roughly 25 contracts across six sectors (energy, precious metals, base metals, grains/oilseeds, softs, livestock), with crude/RBOB/heating oil close to a single bet and gold/silver co-moving on real rates. Man Group's principal-components work finds traditional trend portfolios derive returns primarily from **four macro factors — growth, inflation, monetary policy, trade** ([Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/trend-following-optimal-market-mix)). Putting those together gives an **inferred four-to-eight effective independent bets in a commodity-only book — an inference from sector structure plus Man Group's decomposition, not a computed figure from any source.** With per-bet Sharpe around 0.3–0.5, a well-built commodity-only book should target something like **0.7–1.0 gross and materially less net**. Any backtest showing a commodity-only Sharpe well above that should be assumed to contain a methodology error — most likely percentage returns computed on a back-adjusted series, look-ahead in the roll rule, or survivorship in the contract universe. That is a concrete, pre-committable gate you can add today. Commodity–equity correlation also rose from roughly **−0.10 to about +0.60 at the one-year horizon** between the pre-2005 and 2005–2014 periods, so commodities are less independent of everything else than they were.

Now the part that determines whether any of this is reachable, and it is the methodology, not the signals. A commodity research system needs six things an equity daily-bar stack does not have, and Alpha Lab has none of them. **The per-contract price panel must be the source of truth** — keyed on `(symbol, contract_month, date)` with settlement rather than close as the field that matters — because everything else is derived and a continuous series is unrecoverable back to the panel. **Roll timing, splicing and return computation are three separable decisions**, and conflating them is where most retail commodity research dies. Calendar rolls are deterministic and reproducible but crowded, because index roll windows are publicly known; open-interest rolls track where liquidity actually is but **OI is published with a one-day lag, so using same-day OI is look-ahead bias**; volume rolls are noisier and need a k-day confirmation that is itself a free parameter. For physically-delivered contracts the roll calendar must key on **min(First Notice Day, Last Trading Day) minus a buffer**, not on expiry, because FND for many agricultural and metals contracts precedes last trading day and rolling on "expiry" alone puts a backtest into delivery.

Splicing is where the silent catastrophe lives. **Back-adjusted (Panama) series add the roll price difference to all prior history, so absolute price changes — and therefore dollar P&L and ATR-style volatility — are correct everywhere, but historical price levels are fictitious.** Because the adjustment is a cumulative sum of roll gaps, a persistently contangoed market subtracts at every roll, and over twenty or thirty years of natural gas or WTI the cumulative subtraction can **drive the adjusted series through zero**. At that point `log(P)` is undefined, `P_t/P_{t-1} − 1` flips sign and explodes, percentage-return volatility scaling produces garbage, and ratio-based momentum becomes meaningless — and the backtest shows spectacular fake returns around the crossing. **Ratio-adjusted series make percentage returns correct everywhere and can never go negative**, which makes them the right base for return-based signals and volatility estimation, but dollar P&L and tick counts computed from them are wrong. The only structurally correct method is **unadjusted per-contract prices with explicit return chaining**: on each date compute the return of the contract you actually held; on the roll date, the return is the old contract's return over that day, and the switch itself is a *trade*, not a return, charged round-trip costs on both legs, with the new contract's return starting the following day. Roll return is not an additive term you bolt on — it is already embedded in each contract's convergence toward spot, and adding an explicit "roll yield" double-counts. Chain those per-contract returns into a total-return index and compute Sharpe, volatility and drawdown on that index, never on a price series. A useful invariant test: **the total-return index built from the unadjusted panel must equal the ratio-adjusted continuous series up to a constant multiplier, to floating-point tolerance.** If it does not, the roll calendars disagree. Maintain both views simultaneously; any system with only one is wrong somewhere. Treat vendor "continuous" series as untrusted, because the roll rule is usually undocumented and back-adjustment is frequently the default. (The mathematics here is derivable and presented as method — **no primary exchange or vendor source formally documenting the Panama adjustment and its negative-price failure mode was located**; the available sources are practitioner blogs and library documentation.)

Costs must be **modelled per contract in ticks, not in basis points of notional** — the single biggest departure from equity research, since a one-tick spread is a completely different cost in ES versus corn versus live cattle. The components are exchange plus clearing plus regulatory fees (fixed dollars per contract per side), broker commission (fixed dollars, scaling with contract count not notional), the half-spread expressed as ticks times tick value, slippage as a function of order size over ADV, and **roll cost, which has no equity analogue: every roll is a round trip, so twelve rolls a year means twenty-four legs of spread annually even if the strategy never changes its view**. Where the exchange lists a calendar spread as a single instrument it is often tighter than the sum of the outrights, and the research system must be able to represent that. Financing and collateral interest must be modelled separately from the futures return, since Gorton and Rouwenhorst attribute much of the 2005–2014 decline precisely to zero T-bill yields. The academic cost assumptions available — **EDHEC's 4.4 bp half-spread** and **Fernandez-Perez's 8.6 bp proportional** — apply only to the most liquid handful of contracts and are optimistic for anything below the top fifteen. Make a **cost sensitivity sweep at 2×, 3× and 5× the assumed spread a standard backtest output; if the Sharpe dies at 3×, the result is a cost artefact.** Note as a stated gap that **specific current CME tick sizes, tick values, commission and exchange-fee figures could not be verified** — the spec-page fetches returned marketing copy without the specification tables — and **no reliable published dataset of typical bid-ask in ticks by commodity contract exists**; both must be pulled live from CME/ICE or measured from your own top-of-book data, never taken from memory.

Capital accounting is the other conceptual break. A futures position requires initial margin, a performance bond typically a low single-digit percentage of notional; you do not "buy" the contract, so there is no natural "amount invested." **Three quantities must be tracked separately and never conflated: notional exposure (contracts × contract size × price, which drives P&L and risk), margin requirement (an exchange SPAN constraint plus broker multiplier, not a denominator), and trading capital / NAV (the return denominator and vol-target base).** Leverage is total notional over NAV, routinely 2–5× gross for a diversified CTA at 10–15% vol. Because exchanges reset margin sharply in crises — exactly when you least want it — **margin must be modelled as a time-varying constraint that can force deleveraging**, or the backtest will simply miss forced-liquidation episodes. Position sizing then runs through Moskowitz-Ooi-Pedersen's canonical rule, **position size = 40% / σ, with σ from exponentially-weighted lagged squared daily returns**, equal-weighted across instruments — with three multiplicative terms equity research never needs (contract size, FX rate, and the requirement that σ be estimated on ratio-adjusted or per-contract-chained returns, never on the back-adjusted price), then sector or cluster risk parity because equal-risk-weighting across contracts over-concentrates in whichever sector lists the most contracts, then a portfolio vol target computed with the **full covariance matrix** rather than by summing individual vols, then a constraints layer including integer-contract rounding. **No primary source for a typical CTA portfolio-level vol target number was found**, so do not quote one.

The contract-specification traps round it out, and the canonical event is instructive. On **20 April 2020 the May NYMEX WTI contract traded to an intraday low of −$40.32 and settled at −$37.63**, the first negative settlement in the contract's 37-year history, having opened at $17.73 — a **$58.05 intraday decline** — on the penultimate trading day, driven by physical delivery constraints at Cushing, which was about **76% of capacity by 17 April** with most remaining space already committed; CME removed price floors to permit sub-zero trading and **switched from Black-Scholes to the Bachelier options pricing model effective 22 April 2020**, because log-normal models cannot handle negative underlying prices ([CFTC Interim Staff Report](https://www.cftc.gov/media/5296/InterimStaffReportNYMEX_WTICrudeOil/download); [CME Clearing Advisory 20-171](https://www.cmegroup.com/notices/clearing/2020/04/Chadv20-171.html)). The exchange's own model switch is the precedent for the engineering rule: **no code path may assume `price > 0`.** Add to that per-market contract months (grains trade H/K/N/U/Z while energies trade all twelve, so "roll monthly" is wrong for most of the universe), per-exchange trading calendars (a single forward-filled global date index manufactures fake zero-return days and biases volatility estimates downward), daily price limits (**when a market is locked limit you cannot trade and the printed settlement is not a tradeable price**, so the system needs a derived limit-lock flag), physical-versus-cash settlement flags, and a point-in-time spec table because contracts get re-specified and delisted.

And then the practical wall. Micro contracts are **one-tenth the size of the benchmark** — Micro WTI adjusts exposure in 100-barrel increments ([CME](https://www.cmegroup.com/markets/energy/crude-oil/micro-wti-crude-oil.contractSpecs.html)) — but micros exist for only a subset of commodities (crude, gold, silver, copper; not most agriculturals), so a "micro CTA" is structurally energy-and-metals concentrated, which destroys the sector diversification that is the entire point. FCA permanent restrictions on retail CFDs, effective **1 August 2019**, cap leverage between **30:1 and 2:1**, mandate **margin close-out at 50%** of required margin and **negative balance protection**, and explicitly state that "references to CFDs include financial spread bets" — so spread betting sits inside the same regime ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-confirms-permanent-restrictions-sale-cfds-and-cfd-options-retail-consumers)). The **per-asset-class leverage tiering could not be verified from a primary source**. CFDs and spread bets solve the granularity problem and give you negative balance protection, and the broker is your counterparty quoting a marked-up synthetic price with daily financing that is punitive at mid-frequency holding periods. But the decisive objection is structural, not cost: **UK CFD and spread-bet accounts typically expose only a front-month synthetic price, with no curve access — which means basis, carry and basis-momentum, the three best-evidenced commodity signals in the table above, cannot be computed from the instrument you are trading.** You would need a separate futures-curve data source to generate the signal and then express it through a front-month-only instrument, introducing a basis mismatch between signal and implementation that no amount of validation rigour repairs. Commodity ETFs are long-only, so you cannot express a short and therefore cannot run either momentum family properly, and they carry structural roll decay in contangoed markets. **No published minimum-account-size figure for retail commodity futures from an authoritative source was found, and none should be invented**; the correct framing is that **the binding constraint is contract granularity relative to the per-market risk budget, not the margin requirement.** Note also that **UK tax treatment of spread bets, CFDs and direct futures was not verified against primary HMRC sources** — the available material is broker and accountancy commentary, and the relevant manuals (CG56000-series, BIM22000-series) were not consulted.

## Free data has inverted: crypto hands you dated contracts, the CME complex does not

The free-data landscape now favours crypto in exactly the dimension that matters for methodology, and has degraded for commodities in exactly the dimension that matters for signals. Two named breakages define the commodity picture. Nasdaq Data Link states plainly that **"the CHRIS database has been deprecated and is no longer updated,"** with the rationale that "free data feeds may be deprecated for various reasons" and, critically, **"At this time, we do not currently have an alternate data feed"** ([Packt cookbook issue #5](https://github.com/PacktPublishing/Python-for-Algorithmic-Trading-Cookbook/issues/5), issue opened 20 September 2024). Note the trap that the CHRIS dataset *URLs* still load even though the data is stale, so "the page works" is not evidence the feed is live. Separately, **as of March 2026 Stooq requires an API key**, silently breaking every `pandas-datareader` Stooq pipeline with a `ParserError` — the fourth Stooq-related breakage filed against that project ([pandas-datareader issue #1012](https://github.com/pydata/pandas-datareader/issues/1012)) — and Stooq's bulk-download path is **robots.txt-disallowed**, so it should be treated as having left the free-and-licence-clean category entirely. **Which other Nasdaq Data Link free tables survive in 2026 could not be enumerated**, because the site is fully JavaScript-rendered.

What remains free for commodities is positioning and fundamentals rather than price. **CFTC Commitments of Traders is the most licence-clean, longest-history, most reliable free dataset in the entire catalogue** — US federal work product, compressed ZIPs in text and Excel, Futures Only from **1986**, Futures-and-Options Combined from **March 1995**, Disaggregated from **September 2009**, Traders in Financial Futures from **20 July 2010**, Commodity Index Trader Supplement from **January 2006**, with no explicit licensing restrictions on the page ([CFTC Historical Compressed](https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/CommitmentsofTraders/HistoricalCompressed/index.htm)). EIA's v2 API is free with a registration key, with hard response caps of **5,000 rows for JSON and 300 for XML**, all values returned as strings, and real but unpublished rate limits enforced by temporary key suspension ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/opendata/documentation.php)). USDA Quick Stats is **robots.txt-disallowed to automated fetchers**, so its key requirement, record cap and terms are **unverified — do not assert numbers for it**. CME's own website settlement data is free to *view* after midnight CT, but the programmatic route is paid **DataMine from $105/month**, and "freely available to view" is not a licence for programmatic reuse; **CME's Data Terms of Use could not be retrieved**, which for a risk-averse build should be read as "assume not licensed" ([CME Settlement Data FAQ](https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/faqs/access-to-cme-group-settlement-data-faq.html)).

That produces the asymmetry worth acting on. **Is there a free source of individual dated futures contracts for the CME complex? No.** The free daily sources — Yahoo, Stooq, Investing.com, TurtleTrader — serve pre-built continuous series with undocumented roll methodology, which is precisely the failure mode this report spends a section warning about. The one exception found is **Cboe's CFE, which publishes price and volume detail for select futures products from 2013 to current** ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/us/futures/market_statistics/historical_data/)), making VIX futures the one exchange-traded complex where a free, self-rolled term structure is buildable — though **the exact CSV URL pattern, field list, per-expiry granularity and terms of use could not be confirmed**. Crypto inverts this entirely: **Binance COIN-M quarterly futures under `data/futures/cm/` are individual dated contracts with full bulk history, free**, which means a crypto research system can control its own roll methodology at zero cost. That is a genuine structural advantage of crypto over commodities for a free-data build and it is the hinge of the recommendation below.

Binance's bulk archive is the only free crypto source architecturally suited to a production pipeline: spot, USD-M and COIN-M futures in daily and monthly zips with `.CHECKSUM` companions, "all symbols are supported," intervals from 1s to 1mo, new daily data the next day and monthly on the first Monday ([binance-public-data](https://github.com/binance/binance-public-data)). It carries a live landmine: **"The timestamp for SPOT Data from January 1st 2025 onwards will be in microseconds"** — a silent unit change mid-series. The `data/futures/um/monthly/fundingRate/` and `data/futures/um/daily/metrics/` prefixes exist and are the best free funding and open-interest source available, though **the `metrics` retention window could not be verified** and is a community concern, and **mark-price, index-price, premium-index, book-ticker and liquidation prefixes could not be confirmed to exist — do not assert them.** Everything else is paginated REST: **Binance klines cap at 1,000 per request (weight 2, no key required)**; **OKX at 100 per request at 20 requests per 2 seconds**; **Bybit at 1,000**; **Kraken's REST OHLC returns only 720 most recent entries with older data unretrievable**, offset by free quarterly bulk OHLCVT CSVs; **Hyperliquid serves only the most recent 5,000 candles**, which means 3.5 days of 1-minute data but roughly 13.7 years of daily — **Hyperliquid fine-grained history cannot be backfilled and must be captured incrementally starting now**. **Coinbase Exchange's candles endpoint documents four required auth headers**, so it cannot be part of a no-key pipeline as documented. **Earliest history dates on data.binance.vision could not be verified** (JavaScript-rendered listing, S3 XML blocked by the proxy) and **should not be stated**; neither should Binance's numeric IP rate limit, which lives on a page not retrieved.

The worst-served requirement is delisted-coin history, and the news is bad and specific. CoinGecko, verbatim: **"No, you will not be able to access historical data for inactive or delisted coins via the CoinGecko API"** — at any tier — with coins auto-deactivated after **30 days without trading activity** ([CoinGecko Support](https://support.coingecko.com/hc/en-us/articles/23190618031385-Can-we-access-historical-data-for-inactive-or-delisted-coins-via-CoinGecko-API)). They publish research on dead coins, so they hold the data; they will not serve it. **No free, clean, dated, machine-readable cross-venue delisting register exists.** The workable free substitute is to derive the delisting universe from the exchange archive itself: enumerate every symbol directory in `data.binance.vision/data/spot/monthly/klines/`, compare against the live `exchangeInfo` symbol list, and treat "present in archive, absent from exchangeInfo" as delisted with the last monthly file dating it to month resolution. Binance's own documented example URL is `ADABKRW`, a Korean-won pair that no longer trades, which demonstrates the archive retains delisted symbols. That is survivorship-correct for Binance-listed assets and costs nothing — but it captures *exchange delisting*, not *project death*, so a token that migrated chains or never listed on Binance stays invisible. Because CoinGecko auto-deactivates at 30 days, a self-maintained daily snapshot of `/coins/list` builds your own delisting series going **forward only**. Start snapshotting immediately; you cannot buy back the past.

On tooling, **ccxt is MIT-licensed, covers more than 100 venues, and is very actively developed** (roughly 100,307 commits on master at fetch), which makes it the clear choice with no copyleft exposure ([ccxt](https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt)). **pandas-datareader should be avoided as a dependency** given the Stooq breakage pattern. **OpenBB relicensed to AGPL on 15 May 2024**, meaning anyone who modifies and distributes it or hosts it as SaaS needs a commercial licence unless they release source; material dated 25 August 2026 indicates a move back to a permissive licence for the product suite, but **whether that relicensing has shipped, and under which licence, is unconfirmed** ([OpenBB](https://openbb.co/blog/license-change-openbb-platform-goes-agpl/)). **yfinance is actively maintained, but library maintenance and Yahoo's terms are separate questions** — treat it as a convenience tool, not a licence-clean production source; its licence was not confirmed in this pass. The governing lesson across all of it: **the failure mode of free data is silent format or access change, not outright shutdown.** Checksum-verify, assert on schema, and alert on parse failure rather than trusting a loader.

## CCXT is fine, testnets prove plumbing, and MetaMask is the wrong tool entirely

For market data and, optionally, execution, CCXT is the default abstraction: a unified API across JS/TS/Python/C#/PHP/Go/Java/Rust, MIT-licensed, with a unified `setSandboxMode(true)` that must be called before any other call, client-side rate limiting via `enableRateLimit` (on by default, leaky-bucket with an optional window limiter), a `paginate: true` option, and `fetchPositions` in the unified private surface ([ccxt](https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt); [CCXT Manual](https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt/wiki/Manual)). **There is a genuine conflict in CCXT's own sources about whether WebSocket support is bundled.** A GitHub issue is titled "CCXT Pro Websockets merged with CCXT!" ([issue #15171](https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt/issues/15171)) and the docs site lists WebSocket streaming alongside the unified REST API with a Pro section under the same docs tree ([docs.ccxt.com](https://docs.ccxt.com/)), while the older wiki Manual still reads as though WebSockets are a separate CCXT Pro offering. The wiki appears stale relative to the merge, but **treat "Pro is bundled into the MIT package" as likely-but-verify** before depending on it. Two limitations matter for a research lab. The unified layer is lowest-common-denominator by construction, so exotic order types, portfolio-margin state, sub-account routing and exact funding-interval semantics fall through to `params` passthrough — you write venue-specific code anyway, with extra indirection. And deep historical backfill is CCXT's classic weak spot: venue caps mean multi-year pulls are `since`-stepped loops, and several venues simply do not serve multi-year minute candles publicly at all. **Backfill once from venue bulk archives and use CCXT for incremental top-up.** One operational trap: because `enableRateLimit` is client-side and per-instance, a naive multi-process deployment with three workers against one key will silently exceed the venue's account-level limit; centralise rate limiting at the key boundary.

Testnets are for plumbing and nothing else, and the venues say so. Bybit's own documentation states demo trading is "mainly for trading experience purpose" and **"does not have a complete function compared with the real trading service,"** with demo orders retained 7 days and default rate limits that cannot be upgraded; critically, **private streams are demo but public market data comes from mainnet** ([Bybit](https://bybit-exchange.github.io/docs/v5/demo)). That pattern is the general shape: the price series is real, but the book you trade against is either a thin, mostly bot-driven synthetic book or a simulation against mainnet prices. **Neither reproduces queue position, partial fills or your own impact.** Testnet funding rates are computed off the testnet's own distorted book, so **carry economics cannot be validated on testnet at all** — a material limitation for exactly the crypto strategies with the strongest economic story. **No venue makes a published claim about fill realism, and the absence of such claims is itself the finding.** Hyperliquid's testnet is `api.hyperliquid-testnet.xyz` with an official Python SDK ([Hyperliquid docs](https://hyperliquid.gitbook.io/hyperliquid-docs/for-developers/api)). **Binance, OKX and Deribit testnet documentation was not retrieved in this research** and their endpoints and behaviour should be confirmed from primary docs before being relied on. The honest paper-trading design is therefore not a venue testnet at all: it is a **local paper broker consuming real live data, applying your own fill model and fee schedule, maintaining a shadow portfolio** — with the venue testnet used only once, to prove the signing and order path works before it is ever pointed at mainnet.

API key security has a small number of controls that do nearly all the work, and one asymmetry that should govern the whole design. **Withdrawal permission is never enabled on a programmatic key, ever.** That single choice converts the worst case from "funds gone" to "positions churned," costs nothing, and no systematic system has a legitimate need for it. IP allowlisting is second, and is the reason to run execution from a fixed-IP host rather than a laptop or a dynamic-IP cloud function. But note that even with withdrawal disabled, an attacker with trade permission can destroy an account through **contra-trading** — opening deliberately loss-making positions against their own orders on an illiquid pair — which is the documented 3Commas failure mode, where exchange API keys held by a third-party bot platform were used to drain accounts ([wublock, a secondary aggregator source](https://wublock.substack.com/p/review-of-the-whole-process3commas); no primary post-mortem was located). Defences are trading-pair allowlists where the venue supports them, sub-accounts holding only working capital, venue-side position limits, and balance-change alerting. Keys belong in a secrets manager or a `0600` environment file outside the repository tree, injected at runtime; the three specific anti-patterns are committing a `.env` with live keys, baking keys into a Docker image layer, and pasting keys into a notebook that gets committed with outputs. Add `gitleaks` as a pre-commit hook and enable push protection ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/concepts/secret-security/secret-leakage-risks)). A 2026 GitHub security incident prompted public warnings to rotate crypto API keys ([BeInCrypto](https://beincrypto.com/github-incident-crypto-api-keys/)), but **all available accounts of it are crypto-media secondary sources and no primary GitHub statement was located** — treat it as reported, not established. And **no rigorous, primary-sourced quantification of aggregate losses from leaked exchange API keys exists.** The correct posture for a research-only lab is a **read-only key with IP allowlist**: the blast radius of a leak becomes information disclosure rather than fund loss.

Which brings us to MetaMask, and the answer is short. **MetaMask is a browser-extension wallet: it holds keys locally and exists so a human can review and approve each transaction in a UI.** There is no supported headless mode. People who "automate MetaMask" are driving a browser with Selenium or Playwright and auto-clicking the confirm button, which is fragile, breaks on every extension update, and — decisively — **defeats the only security property MetaMask provides, which is human review.** It should not appear in a server-side design at any point. MetaMask's own security guidance says as much for automated agents: keys that an agent can read "from config files, environment variables, or memory turn any compromise into fund loss," and **"A private key should live somewhere the agent's own process and tools can't read."** Their recommended model puts the key in a trusted execution environment the agent cannot access, with the agent holding credentials only to *request* signatures — **"The agent proposes; it never possesses"** — plus network, address and recipient allowlists, rolling 24-hour outflow limits, per-transaction and rolling spending caps, pre-signing threat simulation, and the explicit warning that **"Policy enforcement outside the model — system prompts aren't sufficient security controls"** ([MetaMask, Agentic wallet security](https://metamask.io/news/agentic-wallet-security)). They also name prompt injection — instructions hidden in token descriptions, web pages or API responses — as an agent-specific threat, which is directly relevant to an LLM-assisted lab.

What a server-side system uses instead, ordered weakest to strongest: a **raw private key in the process** (env var or secrets manager, signing via `web3.py` or `eth-account`) — simplest, most common, and the one where anyone achieving code execution, reading the environment, obtaining a core dump or compromising a dependency takes the entire wallet instantly and irreversibly, with no chargeback and no support desk; the same **raw key plus strict capital segregation**, where the hot key controls only a small float and the bulk sits cold or on a venue, which does not reduce the probability of compromise but is by far the most effective cheap control on loss given compromise; **delegated or session keys with scoped permissions** — Hyperliquid's API/agent wallet model, approved via `approveAgent`, where a separate key can trade but is structurally unable to withdraw and can be revoked, which is the closest on-chain analogue to a no-withdrawal exchange key and the right default for perp-DEX automation ([Hyperliquid API wallets](https://app.hyperliquid.xyz/API)); a **hardware signer**, which protects the key from extraction but still wants a physical button press per signature and therefore does not really solve unattended operation; and **MPC or threshold signing via a custody service**, where the key never exists whole and policy is enforced outside your process — what institutions use, at a cost and vendor-risk trade-off hard to justify for a small book (**pricing and minimum thresholds for small accounts were not researched**).

The asymmetry deserves stating plainly because it should decide the architecture: **with a no-withdrawal exchange API key, the worst case is a churned account. With a hot private key, the worst case is total, instant, irreversible loss of everything that address controls — including funds sent to it afterwards.** Those are different risk categories, not different points on a scale. And for a lab with no execution path the whole branch is deferrable: if on-chain data is needed for research, a read-only RPC provider or a subgraph requires no key at all. **Never create the hot wallet until a strategy demands it.** On DEX execution more broadly, the economics are poor at your size: AMM swaps carry a *fixed* gas cost that is lethal for small clips on L1, L2s fix the gas but thin the liquidity, and MEV does not go away with cheaper gas — any swap broadcast to a public mempool announces its intent and slippage tolerance, and extraction has migrated into private channels rather than disappearing ([EigenPhi data via Cointelegraph](https://www.tradingview.com/news/cointelegraph:fa12ba092094b:0-exclusive-data-from-eigenphi-reveals-that-sandwich-attacks-on-ethereum-have-waned/); [arXiv 2512.17602](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.17602v1)). The structural point: on an AMM there is no queue, no maker rebate, no post-only order, no resting limit — **at mid-frequency you are always a taker**, which is a strictly worse position than a CEX where you can rest passive orders and earn the maker side. Perp DEXes with real order books are the sensible middle ground if on-chain exposure is wanted at all. (**No current gas-cost or MEV-extraction figures for 2026 were obtainable, and perp-DEX fee schedules were available only from commercial, affiliate-adjacent comparison sites.**)

For non-crypto futures, the UK retail default is Interactive Brokers with **`ib_async`, the actively-maintained successor to the now-unmaintained `ib_insync`** — renamed under a new GitHub organisation after the original creator's death in early 2024, requiring Python 3.10+, implementing the IBKR binary protocol so the `ibapi` package is not needed ([ib-api-reloaded/ib_async](https://github.com/ib-api-reloaded/ib_async)); `pysystemtrade`'s community has explicitly discussed the migration ([discussion #1577](https://github.com/robcarver17/pysystemtrade/discussions/1577)). The architectural difference from crypto is large: **IBKR's retail API is not a cloud REST/WebSocket service.** You run a local TWS or headless IB Gateway process that holds the authenticated session, and your code talks to it over localhost — a stateful, GUI-descended process needing daily restart, re-authentication and monitoring (IBC is the standard automation), which effectively mandates a persistent VPS and rules out serverless deployment. IBKR requires **USD 500 minimum equity for market data subscriptions** (USD 200 for certain account types) and waives subscription fees when a per-service commission threshold is met, which means an active account may pay nothing while a research-only account pays in full monthly ([IBKR Market Data Pricing](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/en/pricing/market-data-pricing.php)). **The specific CME market-data prices extracted in this research are suspect and must not be published**: the fetch returned a USD 145.00/month figure in the non-professional column alongside a USD 1.55 L1 figure, which looks like a column-alignment artefact of page extraction, since 145 is closer to typical *professional* bundle pricing. Verify directly against the UK entity before quoting anything. **IBKR account-opening minimums, and the professional-versus-non-professional classification criteria for a UK individual running a systematic system, were not verified** — the latter matters, because business use can trigger materially higher professional data rates. Market data is the recurring fixed cost and it is per-exchange, so a CME-plus-ICE-plus-LME book accumulates subscriptions fast; for research, delayed or end-of-day data suffices and there is no reason to pay for real-time until an execution path exists.

The architecture that ties all of this together is a one-way, narrow interface, and it is the same containment logic as "never enable withdrawal" and "the agent proposes, it never possesses," applied one level up. **Layer one is data**: read-only credentials or none, freely re-fetchable, failure mode is stale data. **Layer two is research**: backtests, fits, signal generation, reading from the data store, holding *no* exchange credentials of any kind, emitting a timestamped file or database row of target positions per instrument with a strategy and version tag; failure mode is a bad number in a file. **Layer three is execution**: the only component holding trading credentials, reading the target, querying actual positions, computing the difference, applying risk constraints and emitting orders, containing no strategy logic whatsoever; failure mode is contained by its own limits. The interface must be **target positions (state), not orders (events)**, because state is idempotent and self-healing — if the executor crashes, misses a fill or restarts, it re-reads target, re-queries actual and converges, whereas an event interface double-fires on replay and drifts silently when a fill is missed. That is the single most important design property for an operation with no ops team. Risk constraints belong in the execution layer as hard gates that reject rather than clamp: maximum gross and net notional, maximum position per instrument, participation cap as a fraction of recent volume, maximum orders per interval to contain runaway loops, **maximum deviation of target from previous target as a sanity check on the research output**, an instrument allowlist, a staleness check refusing to act on a target file older than N minutes, and a kill switch that is a single file checked every loop with a manual flatten-all path. Every decision — target, actual, delta, order sent, fill received — goes to an append-only store, and position reconciliation runs continuously with any mismatch halting trading rather than self-correcting. The security payoff is the real argument: research code is what you iterate on daily, install random packages into, run in notebooks and occasionally paste from the internet, and under this boundary it **literally cannot place an order, because it has no key and no network path to a private endpoint**. A research bug becomes a bad CSV, not a liquidated account. (This architecture is reasoned engineering guidance, visible in open-source frameworks like `pysystemtrade` — **no primary write-up from a named systematic shop describing it explicitly was located.**)

Which yields the clean answer to "should the lab connect to exchanges?" **Not yet, and the architecture is what makes "not yet" a real answer rather than an excuse.** Build layers one and two, have research emit target positions, and point layer three at a paper broker consuming real live data. You get the entire operational loop — target generation, reconciliation, position accounting, P&L attribution, alerting — with zero credentials, zero custody and zero regulatory exposure. Swapping the paper broker for a real one later is one adapter behind an unchanged interface, and by then you have a track record of the target-generation process working end to end. The narrowness of the interface is precisely what makes that swap safe.

## Build the roll engine on free crypto futures, then point it at commodities

The recommendation follows from three facts that point the same way. The mechanisms with durable evidence are in commodities. The free data that lets you *build and validate* futures methodology is in crypto. And your binding constraint is not the market — it is that your gates work and nothing has passed them, which changing asset class does not fix.

**First, and before anything else, close the four cheap gaps in the validation layer, because they apply to every market and they raise the bar your existing discoveries have to clear.** Replace walk-forward with combinatorial purged cross-validation to get a Sharpe *distribution* rather than a point estimate; it is compute-bound and you already have purging and embargoing. Add the CFM Overfitting Factor as a default 2× Sharpe haircut applied *before* the DSR gate, with the composite rule that a candidate needs roughly Sharpe 1.4 backtest to be a credible 0.7 live. Add a correlation-to-existing-library gate so the factory cannot keep promoting variants of what you already own. Add Maven's lagged-signal test as a standard output on every candidate, because it simultaneously measures decay and tells you whether a signal is a latency race you will lose. All four are days of work, none needs new data, and together they are worth more than any change of market.

**Second, add the two evaluation corrections that are specific to the new asset classes and that your current gates would not catch.** Log-wealth profitability testing, because under fat tails a portfolio can show a significant positive mean return with negative cumulative log return, and your DSR deflates a Sharpe computed on the wrong statistic. And a cost sensitivity sweep at 2×, 3× and 5× assumed costs as a mandatory backtest output, with a pre-committed rule that a Sharpe dying at 3× is a cost artefact. Also pre-commit the structural ceiling as a gate: **any commodity-only backtest showing Sharpe materially above 1.0 gross should be treated as evidence of methodology error, most likely percentage returns on a back-adjusted series, look-ahead in the roll rule, or contract-universe survivorship** — not as a discovery.

**Third, build the futures data model and roll engine, and build it on Binance COIN-M quarterly contracts and Cboe VX, because those are free and dated.** The seven components are a per-contract OHLCV+OI panel keyed on `(symbol, contract_month, date)` with settle as the field of record; a point-in-time contract-specification table with effective-date ranges; per-exchange trading calendars; a parameterised, versioned roll engine emitting a stored `roll_schedule` artefact; dual continuous views generated on demand, ratio-adjusted for signals and per-contract chained for P&L, with the invariant test that they agree to a constant multiplier; a tick-denominated per-contract cost model with explicit roll-cost accounting; and margin, notional and NAV tracked as three separate quantities with margin as a time-varying constraint. Build it against crypto dated futures where the data is free and complete, prove the invariant test passes, prove the roll-rule sensitivity sweep runs, and only then decide whether to pay CME DataMine's $105/month for the licence-clean commodity panel. This sequencing is the highest-value idea in this report: **it lets you do the expensive engineering with free data and deliver the bill only once the engineering works.**

**Fourth, resolve access before doing any more strategy research, because access eliminates whole strategy classes before signals do.** For crypto the FCA derivatives ban means the funding, basis and perp-momentum family is reachable only through venues with no UK consumer protection, and **the current UK-retail position of each named venue must be checked individually and was not verified here**. For commodities, the decisive question is whether you can reach real exchange contracts at all, because **CFD and spread-bet accounts expose front-month only, and basis, carry and basis-momentum cannot be computed from a front-month synthetic price.** If the implementation route is CFDs or spread bets, you should know now that the three best-evidenced signals are structurally unavailable and that what remains is trend — which is a two-parameter structure, not a search problem. That single determination reshapes the entire research programme, and it costs a phone call rather than a quarter of backtesting.

**Fifth, narrow the search and keep the gates.** This is the uncomfortable one. With four to eight independent bets in commodities and a crypto cross-section whose premia live where prices are least reliable, a genetic-programming refiner over an AST-whitelisted expression space is running a wide search over a narrow universe, which is the exact configuration that inflates trial counts while producing nothing that can survive them. Your own v0.3 ablation already showed it: the GP refiner produced the most statistically strong factors and the weakest survivors. The right shape for these markets is a small number of pre-specified, economically grounded structures — curve slope, curve-slope momentum, price trend, positioning — evaluated hard, with the bandit allocating effort across *implementations* of those structures rather than across an open expression space. Keep the Thompson-sampling allocator; shrink what it allocates over.

**Sixth, defer execution entirely, and when it comes, build it as the third layer described above.** Target-position interface, paper broker first, read-only keys for research, no withdrawal permission ever, no hot wallet until a strategy demands one, and no MetaMask at any point.

What should be *stopped*: chasing crypto cross-sectional momentum in the lab's current cross-sectional shape, on cost arithmetic alone; building any BTC/ETH cash-and-carry capability, since 3.6–6% gross before fees, collateral drag and 10-October-style tail risk is a worse trade than a gilt; and any mean-variance optimisation layer, since without a commercial risk model it converts covariance error into position error with leverage.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing this analysis produces is that Alpha Lab's two halves have diverged in value. The validation half — trial-counted deflation, purged holdouts, the leakage audit, hash-stamped pre-registration, promotion and retirement on out-of-period rechecks — is the part institutions describe in public and mostly fail to enforce in private, and it is fully portable to markets where the data is cheap and the point-in-time moat does not exist. The discovery half is tuned to an arithmetic that does not hold outside a wide equity cross-section, and porting it unchanged to a universe of twenty-five contracts or a hundred perps will produce more trials, better-looking backtests and the same zero survivors, only faster. The honest reading of "no factor has ever passed a fully-counted Deflated Sharpe" is not that the gates are too strict; it is that the gates are the only thing in the system currently doing its job.

The second reframing is about where the real work is. In equities you buy an edge with data. In futures you *build* one with methodology: the difference between a competent and an incompetent commodity backtest is almost entirely roll timing, splicing, return chaining, tick-denominated costs and margin accounting — none of which is a modelling problem, all of which is engineering, and all of which can be built and proven for free on crypto dated contracts before a penny is spent on CME data. That is an unusually favourable configuration for a solo builder, and it is the opposite of the equity situation where the moat is purchased. Set against it, the crypto access picture is the clearest negative result in this report: the mechanisms with real economic content are the ones a UK retail trader cannot reach through a regulated firm, and the ones that remain reachable have been compressed to a formula floor by several billion dollars of institutional carry capital. A strategy you cannot legally be sold, in a market whose flagship trade now pays less than the collateral, is not a research programme. Commodity carry is — and it has paid roughly the same 10% a year through two independent sample periods spanning fifty-five years, which is more than can be said for anything else in this document.
